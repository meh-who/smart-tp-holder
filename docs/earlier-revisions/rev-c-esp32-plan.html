Why it's worth doing at all

The 20,000 mAh column is a joke you should ignore — the point is that 2000 mAh, a cell the size of a credit card and half a centimetre thick, gives you a quarter of a year. The Pi needs a pack forty times heavier to manage five days. That's not a tuning difference, it's a different class of object: one you hang on a wall and forget, versus one with a charging habit attached.

Decision one
02

Light sleep, not deep sleep

I told you last time this would run on deep sleep at 18 µA. Having worked through the wake path, that's wrong, and it's worth showing why because the mistake is seductive.

Deep sleep powers down the CPU and most of RAM. Waking means the ROM bootloader runs, the app is re-loaded from flash, LittleFS remounts, I2S re-initialises — before a single sample reaches the amp:

Deep sleep wake pathmscumulative
RTC wake + ROM bootloader140140
2nd-stage bootloader + app load from flash110250
LittleFS mount90340
I2S + PCNT init12352
Open file, decode first MP3 frame14366
MAX98357A un-mute settling30396
Total396 ms

That's twice the 200 ms budget the entire product is built around. Deep sleep would buy world-class battery life and hand back a device that feels broken on every single pull — trading away the only thing that makes the interaction work.

Light sleep keeps RAM powered. The filesystem stays mounted, I2S stays configured, execution resumes at the next instruction:

Light sleep wake pathmscumulative
GPIO edge wake11
RAM retained — no remount, no re-init01
Open file, decode first MP3 frame1415
Amp already woken at PULL_STARTED, 33 mm earlier015
Total15 ms

250 µA instead of 18 µA — about 6 mAh a day, a third of the total budget. Worth every microamp. Deep sleep still appears in the firmware, but only after three hours of no use, where 400 ms on the first pull of the morning is genuinely unnoticeable.

The knock-on effect

A 250 µA sleep budget has a consequence the Pi build never had to think about: you cannot have a boost converter. A typical 5 V boost module idles at 10–20 mA, which is forty to eighty times the entire sleep budget. It would dominate everything.

So the ESP32 build has no 5 V rail at all. The board runs from the cell through its own LDO, and the amp runs straight off the cell too — a bridge-tied MAX98357A at 3.7 V still delivers 1.7 W into 4 Ω, and speech in a small tiled room needs well under one. One fewer module, one fewer failure mode, and the sleep budget survives.

Decision two
03

Most of the software doesn't move

The instinct on a platform port is to rewrite everything in C++. Don't. RSS parsing, fuzzy dedupe, the gentle-mode filter and a TLS call to a TTS vendor are all memory-hungry, all change often, and a microcontroller in a bathroom is the worst place on earth to maintain any of them.

So they don't move. newsroom.py and tts.py run unchanged on a server — a Pi already in the house, a NAS, a cheap VPS, or cron pushing to an S3 bucket. They write a static folder. The ESP32 fetches a manifest once an hour and plays files.

Measured, not estimated — I built a real bulletin with the existing pipeline:

Flash and sync budgetmeasured
One headline, 64 kbps mono MP3, 4.3 s34 KB
20 headlines + 4 system clips543 KB
Fits a 6 MB LittleFS partition11× over

543 KB is small enough that there is no SD card in this design. One less connector to corrode in a humid room, and one less mount step in the wake path.

The port
04

Proving the gesture engine survived translation

motion.py is where all the subtlety lives, and a hand-translation of it into C++ is exactly the kind of change that looks correct and isn't. The failure modes are invisible on a read-through: the falsy-zero guard that skips the first speed sample, the reverse-credit clamp, whether last_motion_at survives a PULL_ENDED.

So bs_motion.cpp has no Arduino, ESP-IDF or FreeRTOS dependency at all. It compiles on a laptop, and the same pull traces run through both implementations and get diffed field by field:

Risks
07

What I can't verify from here

The gesture engine is proven. These are the parts that need a board on a bench.

  • PCNT API version. encoder.cpp uses driver/pulse_cnt.h, the ESP-IDF 5.x API that ships with Arduino-ESP32 3.x. On 2.x you need the older driver/pcnt.h and the calls differ. platformio.ini pins the newer one, but this is the first thing that will bite you.
  • Audio library churn. ESP8266Audio's I2S wrapper has moved around across Arduino-ESP32 versions. Everything library-specific hides behind three void* members in player.h, so ESP32-audioI2S is a contained swap if it fights you.
  • The 250 µA light-sleep figure. It's the datasheet-plus-experience number for an S3 with WiFi off and RAM retained. Real dev boards leak — a power LED alone is 2–5 mA, which would be ten to twenty times the sleep budget. Measure it, and be prepared to desolder LEDs and check what the onboard USB-serial chip does when idle.
  • setInsecure() in sync.cpp. Fine fetching your own bulletin off your own LAN. Pin a root CA before it talks to anything you don't control.
  • Counts lost at wake. PCNT doesn't run during light sleep, so the edge that wakes the chip isn't counted. That's 2.7 mm against a 45 mm first trigger — it vanishes into the noise gate, but it's a real known loss rather than something I've hidden.
What to do
08

The order I'd actually do this in

My advice hasn't changed: don't port yet. Build Revision B on the Pi, live with it for a few weeks, and find out whether one-sheet-one-headline is the right interaction or whether depth-into-one-story is. That question is worth more than three months of battery life, and Python on real Linux is where you want to be while it's open.

The ESP32 build is what you do once you've stopped changing your mind. When that happens:

  • Stand up the server first. python3 server/build_bulletin.py --out public --serve on whatever machine is already on in your house. Point a browser at bulletin.json and confirm it looks right. This works today, with no ESP32 in the room.
  • Run the port diff before you flash anything. If the gesture engine is wrong, every symptom above it will mislead you.
  • Bring up audio alone. Board, amp, speaker, one MP3 on LittleFS, no encoder and no sleep. Nothing else matters until this is clean.
  • Then the encoder, then sync, then sleep — in that order. Sleep last, always. Debugging a chip that keeps going to sleep while you're trying to talk to it is its own special misery.
  • Measure the sleep current before you trust any of my numbers. A multimeter in series with the cell, everything idle. If it isn't in the hundreds of microamps, something on that dev board is awake that shouldn't be.
