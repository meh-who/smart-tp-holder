A toilet roll holder that reads the news

Pull a sheet, hear a headline. An encoder counts the paper, a cached voice does the talking, and an e-ink panel prints what it just said. Here is the whole thing — the parts, the wiring, the maths that makes it actually work, and the three problems that are harder than they look.

How it works
01

The signal chain

Everything on the hot path — from hand to sound — is four steps long, and none of them touch the network. That is the entire trick.

The hard parts
02

Problem one: the roll shrinks

This is the one that bites everybody. The obvious build puts the encoder on the spindle and counts degrees per sheet. It works perfectly — for about a week.

A full roll is 110 mm across, so one revolution pays out 346 mm of paper: roughly three sheets. A bare core is 44 mm across, so one revolution pays out 138 mm: barely more than one sheet. The same rotation means two and a half different things depending on when you ask.

What a fixed constant costs you

Over-read of a spindle encoder calibrated once on a full roll · 194-sheet roll

Two ways out

Idler wheel — recommended

Stop measuring the roll; measure the paper. A 20 mm wheel riding on the sheet has a circumference that never changes, so mm-per-count is one constant you calibrate once and forget.

Spindle + radius model

Keep the encoder on the axle and integrate dr/dθ = −t/2π as paper leaves. No extra parts, needs a "new roll" button — and it gives you a sheets-remaining readout the idler can't.

# geometry.py — the spindle model, in four lines
dtheta = d_counts * 2*pi / counts_per_rev
r0     = self.r
r1     = r0 - self.t * dtheta / (2*pi)      # radius shrinks as paper leaves
return 0.5 * (r0 + r1) * dtheta          # mean radius → exact to 2nd order
Free feature

The radius model also tells you how much paper is left: the annulus area divided by paper thickness. That is the 194-sheet figure at the top of this page, and it is what the e-ink panel shows in standby. Nobody needs a toilet roll with a fuel gauge. Everybody wants one.

03

Problem two: speech is slower than hands

This one only shows up once you build it. A three-sheet pull takes about 1.2 seconds. Three spoken headlines take about 13 seconds. Queue them honestly, one per sheet, and the holder is still talking long after the person has finished, washed their hands and left the room.

I measured it on the bench build: one 342 mm pull produced 13.5 s of speech. The hand was done at 1.2 s.

There is no clever fix, only honest levers. The build uses three:

  • A speech backlog cap. audio.max_backlog_s: 20 — past twenty seconds of queued audio the engine refuses new headlines rather than narrate to an empty bathroom.
  • A pull cap. motion.max_queue: 4 — a toddler unspooling the whole roll gets four headlines, not ninety.
  • Shorter source material. Feeds with terse headlines beat feeds with subordinate clauses. This matters more than either cap.
Worth reconsidering

If you build this and find the overlap annoying, the fix is a different interaction, not a better queue: make sheets set the depth of one story rather than the count of stories. One sheet, the headline. Two, headline plus a sentence. Three, the whole piece. Same gesture, and it can never outrun the hand.

04

Problem three: latency is the whole product

If the first word lands more than about 200 ms after the paper starts moving, the thing feels broken, and no amount of voice quality rescues it. Two decisions protect that budget.

Speech is cached, never live. A cloud TTS round trip is 600–2000 ms. So the twelve most likely headlines are synthesized right after each news refresh and normalised to 22 kHz mono WAV, which the Pi can start playing with no decode at all. A pull costs a file read and a buffer append.

The first headline fires early. Waiting for a full 114 mm sheet means the person has already stopped pulling. So the first trigger of a pull is at 45 mm — about 40% of a sheet — and only subsequent ones use the full pitch. Sheet-counting purity loses to feel.

# motion.py
threshold = self.first_trigger_mm if self._pull_sheets == 0 \
            else self.sheet_mm

One persistent sounddevice output stream stays open from boot, so the I2S clock never stops — which also kills the pop the MAX98357A makes when its bit clock starts and stops.

The cheap version

You asked about a mouse scroll wheel, and it does work — the repo ships a QuadratureSensor driver for exactly that, and the cost is zero if you have a dead mouse in a drawer. Two warnings. Prefer a mouse with an optical wheel encoder (an IR gate straddling a slotted disc) over the cheap grey mechanical kind, which bounces and wears out. And if the wheel has a detent spring, take it out — detents make the paper pull feel notchy and add drag right where you don't want it. You also drop from 4096 counts per revolution to about 24, which is coarse but perfectly adequate at these distances.

Power, honestly

A Pi Zero 2 W draws ~120 mA idling and ~350 mA talking, and it has no useful sleep state. A 5000 mAh pack gets you roughly a day and a half. This design wants a wall socket. If a battery is non-negotiable, the platform is wrong — an ESP32-S3 with streamed MP3 will idle for weeks, at the cost of writing the firmware in C++ and giving up offline speech.

07

The mechanical bit

This is where the project is actually won or lost. The electronics are an afternoon; getting a wheel to track paper without tearing it took me longer than the software.

  • Spring pressure is the tuning knob. Too light and the wheel slips, under-counting; too heavy and it drags and the sheet tears at the perforation instead of pulling. Start too light and add tension until calibration runs repeat within 3%.
  • Put the wheel near the tear bar. That is where the paper is flat and in tension. Ride it against the roll surface instead and you are back to a changing diameter, which is the bug you just solved.
  • The magnet must be diametrically magnetised. An axially magnetised one of the same size reads as no magnet at all. The driver checks the AS5600 status register on startup and refuses to run with a bad gap, which saves a lot of guessing.
  • Print in PETG, not PLA. A hot shower repeatedly is not kind to PLA, and a warped idler bracket ruins your calibration.
  • Separate the wet side from the dry side. Electronics in a sealed upper compartment with the speaker firing through a downward-facing slot, so splashes and steam don't settle on the board. A thin coat of conformal spray on the Pi is cheap insurance.
  • Give the speaker a sealed back volume. An open-backed 40 mm driver in a tiled room sounds like a phone in a bucket. Even a small closed chamber behind it helps enormously.
Software
08

Install, then calibrate

# on a fresh Raspberry Pi OS Lite (Bookworm, 64-bit)
sudo bash hardware/setup-pi.sh
sudo reboot

# the step you cannot skip
sudo -u broadsheet /opt/broadsheet/.venv/bin/python \
     /opt/broadsheet/tools/calibrate.py -c /etc/broadsheet/config.yaml

Do not compute mm-per-count from the wheel diameter. The O-ring compresses, the paper slips a little, and your 20 mm wheel is really 19.6 mm. The calibration tool has you pull a measured length three times and prints the constant to paste into the config. If the three runs disagree by more than 3%, the wheel is slipping — add spring tension.

Everything runs on a laptop too, with no hardware at all:

python3 tools/simulate_pull.py   # trace the gesture engine on synthetic pulls
python3 -m pytest tests/ -q      # 16 tests, no Pi required

Design notes
09

Decisions that aren't technical

A talking object in a bathroom is socially strange in ways a spec sheet won't tell you. A few calls I'd defend:

  • It never repeats itself. Spoken headlines are logged with a 12-hour cooldown, so the second person in the morning gets different news from the first. Without this the device is a broken record by day three.
  • Gentle mode is on by default. The blocklist is a blunt regex and it will over-filter. That is the right error to make in this room. It is one config flag away for anyone who disagrees.
  • A short press repeats the last headline. The single most predictable failure is mishearing over running water. Repeat costs one button and one line of code.
  • Failure is quiet, not chatty. No Wi-Fi means it plays the last cached bulletin and says nothing about it. An appliance that announces its own error states in a bathroom is intolerable.
  • The e-ink is the graceful degradation. Quiet hours, guests, a houseguest who hates it — mute the audio and the panel still shows the headline. The thing keeps working with its voice off, which is a good test of whether a voice product is well designed.
  • It holds its last image at zero power. When unplugged it doesn't go blank; it stays a small printed headline on the wall. That is a nicer object than a dark screen.
If you build it again
10

The second version

Three things I'd change knowing what the first build taught me.

Depth, not count. Sheets choosing how deep to go into one story beats sheets choosing how many stories, purely because it cannot outrun the hand. Same gesture, no queue problem, and it rewards a long pull with something better than more of the same.

ESP32-S3 for the shipping version. The Pi is the right prototype — Python, real TTS options, a debugger. But it needs a wall socket, and a battery-powered version that idles for a month is a much better object. The port is a rewrite of audio.py and tts.py around streamed MP3; geometry.py and motion.py translate to C++ almost line for line.

A roll-diameter sensor. One cheap IR reflectance sensor pointed at the roll would re-seed the radius model automatically and retire the "hold the button when you change the roll" step — the one piece of the interaction that asks the user to remember something.
