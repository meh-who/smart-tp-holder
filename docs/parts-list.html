Notes that will save you an evening
On the encoder pack

The 5/7/9/11/13 mm in that listing is the height — mounting plane to wheel axis — not resolution. Your 21 mm wheel has a 10.5 mm radius, so only the 13 mm has real clearance and the 11 mm has none to spare. Effectively you're buying 25 to get about 10 usable, which is still five times cheaper per usable part than the 2-pack.

The listing says "Movement Detection Technology: Optical". It isn't — metal can, hex rotor, contact encoder. The same table claims "Connectivity Technology: USB" for a through-hole component, so treat the whole attribute block as decoration.

Do this before building the arm

Declick one, then flick the hex rotor with a fingernail. A declicked encoder coasts; a detented one stops dead at the next click. Then press a wheel on, hold it against a roll with the pressure of a couple of coins, and drag the paper — it should roll smoothly with no skid.

If that fails across several units, that's your signal to order the AS5600. Don't discover it after the arm is built.

Tools, if you don't have them

  • Fine needle-nose pliers — for bending the encoder can's tabs during the declick.
  • Digital calipers — measure the hex across the flats before you model the wheel hub. It may differ between heights.
  • Multimeter — identify the common pin (almost certainly the middle one) by continuity while rotating slowly.
Why the totals moved

Up $11 on the Pi and $8 on the ESP32 against what the earlier docs said. Two reasons, both bookkeeping: the encoder went from a $0–3 salvaged mouse to the $9.99 pack, and the printed wheel and O-ring is now its own line instead of being folded into "idler wheel". Nothing else changed, and the AS5600 coming off partly offsets it.
